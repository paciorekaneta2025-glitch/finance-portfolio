## What is IRR?

Now it is time for IRR — Internal Rate of Return.

IRR shows the percentage return generated by an investment over time. This could be a project, business, property, startup, or any other investment that generates cash flow.
Every investment has its own “hidden interest rate” — and that hidden rate is exactly what IRR represents.

Simply put:
IRR shows how much percentage return an investment generates after considering:

  * the time value of money, 
  * and all future cash flows. 

But an important question arises:

How does this relate to the Discount Rate?

The Discount Rate is the rate of return that WE expect to receive from an investment. It depends on:

    * risk, 
	* industry, 
	* our expectations, 
	* and how much return we want to achieve. 

We may expect 3%, 10%, or even much more — or much less.

IRR, however, helps us discover the true “hidden return” of the investment.

Technically speaking, IRR is the discount rate at which:

#### NPV=0

This allows us to compare:

    * what we expect from the investment, 
	* with what the investment actually generates. 

It helps us evaluate whether our assumptions and expected rate of return are realistic, too high, or perhaps too low.

Let’s look at this step by step and experiment with IRR in practice.

### How to Calculate IRR Manually

Of course, we can use the ready-made Excel formula:

### =IRR(B2:B18)

But I want to show it manually — in the simplest possible way — so that IRR can even be calculated on a piece of paper while truly understanding what is happening behind the numbers.

Let’s assume that:

  * we invest £400 today, 
  * and then receive £100 per year for 5 years. 

The question is:
**  What percentage return does this investment generate? **

#### Step 1 — We Guess the Interest Rate

When calculating IRR manually, we need to do a bit of “trial and error.”

So, for example, we may assume:

  * 10% 
  * 7%
    
and then check what NPV we get.

#### Step 2 — Let’s test 10%

First, we calculate the Present Value of all future cash flows.

PV=100/(1+10%) +100/(1+10%)^2 +100/(1+10%)^3 +100/(1+10%)^4 +100/(1+10%)^5 

After substituting the values:

=100/1.1+100/1.21+100/1.331+100/1.4641+100/1.61051

We get:

=90.90909+82.644628+75.13148+68.301345+62.092132

PV=379.07867

Now we calculate NPV:

NPV=-400+379.07867=-20.92132

#### Step 3 — Let’s test 7%

Now we try a lower percentage.

PV=100/1.07+100/(1.07)^2 +100/(1.07)^3 +100/(1.07)^4 +100/(1.07)^5 

After calculation:

=93.4579+87.3438+81.62978+76.28952+71.29861

PV=410.019

Now we calculate NPV:

NPV=-400+410.019=10.019

This time NPV is positive.

## Conclusion

Since:

   * at 10% the NPV is negative, 
   * and at 7% the NPV is positive, 

our IRR must be somewhere between 7% and 10%.

In practice:

IRR≈ 7.93% (around 7.9%)

And it is exactly at this interest rate that:

NPV=0

That is why IRR is called the “hidden rate of return” of an investment.

So, we are slowly starting to understand what IRR really is.

Of course, simply reading the theory is not enough — IRR is best understood through practice and experimentation with different cash flows and different discount rates.

It is worth remembering one very important rule:

  * lower Discount Rate → higher Present Value, 
	
  * higher Discount Rate → lower Present Value. 

  
Why?

Because the higher the return we require from an investment, the less future money is worth today.

Then we combine this with our initial investment and begin to see the true picture of the project.

This allows us to evaluate:

   * whether the investment is realistic, 
   * whether our expectations make sense, 
   * whether the project truly creates value, 
   * whether it is just a “dreamer’s idea,” 
   * or simply an investment not worth entering into.
     
And that is exactly why NPV and IRR are so important in finance, investing, and financial modelling.

## Important Note About IRR

Although IRR is a very useful financial metric, it is not always perfect.

In projects with unusual or irregular cash flows, IRR can sometimes produce misleading or unrealistic results.

That is why, in real financial analysis, IRR is often used together with:

- NPV,

- cash flow analysis,

- risk assessment,

- and other financial metrics.
